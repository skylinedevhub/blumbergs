Blumbergs DAF Report: 
Donor Advised Fund accounts in 2024 
 
By Mark Blumberg & Henri Pasha (February 10, 2026) 
Donor Advised Funds (DAFs) have become increasingly popular in Canada. A DAF is a registered charity that 
has separate donor-advised funds. In many cases it does more than just have donor advised funds. 
A donor can donate to the DAF, receive an official donation receipt and then afterwards make recommendations 
to the DAF as to which registered charities should receive gifts from the DAF. Here is our blog directory on DAFs 
with many different articles dealing with DAFs. Also, here is our course on Donor Advised Funds (DAFs) in 
Canada. 
DAFs have many uses, including but not limited to: 
 being a philanthropic tool for donors to do long-term philanthropy, whether it is an individual, non-profit, 
for-profit, etc. 
 a place to park funds if you are establishing a charitable organization, but it is going to take months to get 
your registered charitable status 
 a place to collect funds from different sources for a particular charitable purpose 
 a place to move funds if you are winding down your charity or foundation 
There is much confusion between DAFs and private foundations. A DAF is a DAF and not your own private 
foundation. Make sure before you set up a DAF that you understand all the limitations (and benefits) of a DAF 
or having a DAF may be disappointing for some. 
Also, not all registered charities that have funds that are donor advised necessarily market themselves as DAFs 
or are interested in having individuals provide them with funds to be held as a DAF. 
We would just emphasize that there is a very big difference between the sorts of DAFs that that some community 
foundations, banks and financial services companies may establish and many of the charities that actually do have 
one or more donor advised funds. Another way of looking at it is that there is really is no such thing as an entity 
being a DAF in Canada, there are just registered charities and some of them have donor advised funds that are set 
up in a particular circumstance and others have sophisticated systems that encourage the public to donate even at 
very low levels like $5000. We know that some religious organizations or even groups that fundraise for foreign 
universities are clearly not DAFs in the traditional sense, but they do occasionally take in money and then hold it 
so that a particular donor can give them recommendations later.

Furthermore, there are very few registered charities that only deal with DAFs. Many groups advertising they are 
a DAF do many other things other than just having DAFs. Even the ones that heavily advertise as DAF’s often 
have most revenue and gifts, not necessarily in a DAF but in a restricted gift of another sort. 
When you look at the list it is possible that some of the charities listed may not actually have DAFs or may not 
have as many assets in the DAFs as they suggest. In some cases these are other types of restricted gifts that do 
not have the donor just making a ‘recommendation’. 
So yes this information is from 2024 and many organizations are still not completing the boxes correctly. 
Although in fairness, many charities are not completing correctly boxes that have been on the T3010 for 30 years! 
When we review draft T3010s we find that over 90% of the drafts have significant mistakes in them.  You will 
not many transpositional errors – a group with $31,000 in total DAF assets probably does not have 30,000 DAFs.   
Historically, there has been little information available about DAFs, except for research by Keith Sjogren. 
The CRA started in 2023 to collect information on donor advised funds (DAFs) in Canada as we note in this blog. 
We previously wrote an article regarding Donor Advised funds in 2023, however, only those charities with ending 
December 31st or later filed using Form T3010 using T3010 version 24, which contained the new DAF related 
questions.  
As of fiscal year 2024, all Canadian registered charities have used the new Version 24 of the T3010 which includes 
the DAF questions, so the list below is far more complete with 837 organizations saying they have DAFs, 
compared to the roughly 400 included in 2023. 
The DAF-related questions are: 
1. Did the charity hold any donor advised funds (DAF) during the fiscal period? (Line 5860)  
2. Total number of accounts held at the end of the fiscal period (Line 5861) 
3. Total value of all accounts held at the end of the fiscal period (Line 5862) 
4. Total value of donations to DAF accounts received during the fiscal period (Line 5863) 
5. Total value of qualifying disbursements from DAFs during the fiscal period (Line 5864) 
 
CRA notes in their guide to completing the T3010: 
C18 – Line 5860 – If the charity held any Donor Advised Fund (DAF) during its fiscal period, select yes. 
For the purposes of this guide, a DAF is a fund segregated into donor accounts, owned and controlled by 
a registered charity. Each account is comprised of contributions made by individual donors. Donors may 
provide ongoing non-binding suggestions on payouts from DAFs, but it is the charity's sole responsibility 
to make such decisions.

Here is some of the cumulative statistics from the 2024 data with my comments in brackets: 
 About 834 Canadian registered charities identified having DAFs. 
 # of DAF Accounts is listed as 185,506 [highly exaggerated by transpositional errors, etc.] 
 Total Value DAF accounts is listed as $16,644,279,820 or $16.6 billion. [This approximately 10% of the 
assets of the private and public foundations in Canada and growing] 
 Donations to DAF Accounts were $3,280,632,301 or about $3.2 billion. [This means that about 1 in 10 
dollars donated to the charity sector goes to DAFs and not directly to operating charities] 
 Total Value of Gifts from DAF Accounts is $1,918,665,555 or over $1.9 billion. [About $1.3 billion 
more stayed in DAF accounts than went out in 2024 increasing the size of DAFs assets but 
compounding the difficulty of average operational charities to fundraise and increasing inequality in 
Canada] 
 Gifts as a Percentage of Donations is 58%. [As above it shows the capital appreciation in DAFs is 
because more money is going in than coming out and it does not hurt that investments are not taxed] 
 Total DAF Expenditures as a Percentage of Total DAF Assets is 12% [some are much lower and others 
much higher but some of this is flowthrough during the year. Some of the biggest donations are 
movements from one DAF to another or from a DAF to a private foundation] 
 Average Account Value is $89,723.67 [This reflects some very large DAF accounts and many small 
accounts.  We don’t have the median value of DAFs and therefore don’t know about capital 
concentration or what is the typical DAF account.] 
 
Keep in mind the many caveats about T3010 information, including that registered charities sometimes make 
mistakes when they file their return, and sometimes CRA inputs the information incorrectly. Also keep in mind 
that money coming into DAFs is not necessarily new money for the charitable sector. It is sometimes just money 
coming from another DAF. Also, when money is leaving a DAF, it is not always going to an operating charity 
but may just be going to another DAF or a private foundation. 
Some of the columns like “Total DAF Expenditures as a % of Total DAF Assets” and “DAF Gifts as a % of 
DAF Donations” were calculations that we have made. However, if a particular T3010 question is not answered 
or a zero is inserted, the calculation is not applicable and marked with a “N/A”.

Limitations and Caveats 
There are a number of cautions in dealing with the information from the T3010. You can access the CRA Charities Listing database directly on the CRA website at: www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/charities-giving/list-
charities/list-charities-other-qualified-donees.html. As well, Blumbergs also maintains Canada's largest charity information portal at www.CharityData.ca with up to 20 years of information on every Canadian registered charity. The portal 
is free, and the aim is to increase transparency in the Canadian charity sector. With CRA removing over 10 years’ worth of information from the Charities Listing, www.CharityData.ca has far more years available. Currently, CRA is only 
providing 5 years of historical information on each charity. 
 
1) The data in this note is based on the 2024 T3010 Registered Charity Information Return filings. Although all charities are supposed to file their T3010 within six months of their fiscal year end, some charities file late or do not file at all 
and then will lose their charitable status. Depending on the data set from CRA, not every charity may have filed their T3010 or been inputted yet.  
2) Registered charities complete the T3010, and one person signs the form. The T3010 information is not independently verified by CRA when it is posted on the CRA site or placed in the CRA database. 
3) Although an important legal document, the T3010 is often completed by volunteers or others who may have little understanding of the nuances of the Income Tax Act (Canada), limited language skills, may not have easy access to the 
correct information or are in a hurry to file the form to avoid deregistration. In the case of larger institutions, it is typically accountants or finance staff who prepare the T3010. One would expect greater accuracy with the larger institutions. 
However, as they also tend to be more complicated and involve bigger numbers, the likelihood of a significant inaccuracy in their T3010 filings is great. 
4) The T3010s are filed with CRA and in many cases need to be coded by hand at CRA which can also introduce mistakes. Only a small number of T3010s have 2D bar code technology which can eliminate most processing errors on 
CRA’s part. Canadian charities can, as of June 2019, electronically file the T3010 which does reduce errors and speed up the time that T3010 data is processed and available to the public. All charities should be filing their T3010’s 
electronically using the CRA MyBA system.  
 
5) In some cases, those completing the T3010 for a charity are deliberately deceptive when completing the T3010. For example, an organization knows that it has substantial fundraising expenses but chooses to put them under charitable 
activities. Or an organization claims pharmaceuticals that it can purchase for $50,000 are really worth $50 million. 
6) Don’t rely on any of this information without checking with the charity and appropriate due diligence as required.  
7) The T3010 is a tax form which is supposed to be completed according to guidance provided by the CRA in Guide Completing the Registered Charity Information Return (T-4033) and also various other CRA guidances and policies. The 
information inputted may not conform to Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) and often the numbers will not match what is in a financial statement. You can obtain copies of financial statements, whether audited or not, on 
any registered charity by requesting the financial statements from CRA. These financial statements are filed with CRA but not placed on the CRA website and they can sometimes add further information. Charities are not required to post 
their financial statements on their website, but charities should put up at least the last 5 years financial statements to make it easier for donors and the public to access them. 
8) Some questions on the T3010 are more prone to error than others. Some questions are complicated, while others are quite straightforward. Some matters are easily quantifiable, like value of official donation receipts, while others require 
allocation and other subjective measurements. Some questions, like revenue, are answered by almost every charity and if a charity makes a mistake, the likelihood is that such a mistake will not have much impact on the bigger picture. On 
the other hand, to take a historical example some numbers like political expenditures by Canadian charities are often incorrectly completed and even one or two charities by mistakenly inserting large numbers in this category can distort the 
picture. With the 2012 numbers, the top six organizations listing political expenditures appear to be all mistakes. This seems to have inflated the numbers by about 1000%. You can see my article “Did the University of Windsor spend $285 
million on political activities in 2012”. For 2013, the political numbers were $171 million on the T3010. If one reviews the actual organizations, the number is probably about $26 million. For 2017, the number is $28 million. For 2018, the 
number is $30 million. Unfortunately, today the CRA does not ask any questions about political activities of Canadian charities.  
9) The T3010 asks certain questions. Many important questions are not asked on the T3010. Relying on the T3010 to make decisions on whether a charity is efficient or worthwhile to support is prone to failure. We have established a 
website at www.SmartGiving.ca which discusses in detail questions donors may want to ask before donating to charity.
